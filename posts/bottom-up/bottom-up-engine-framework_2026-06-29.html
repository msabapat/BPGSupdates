Appendix: Commercial Directions — What Could Be Built From the Bottom-Up Engine

Ten candidate products/uses for the multibagger-identification framework above · difficulty, form, market, incumbents, and honest revenue ranges for a two-person shop · with competitive positioning, v0 specs, and go-to-market notes per option · all dollar figures are rough estimates with wide error bars · 2026-07-04

This appendix treats the framework above as raw material and asks: what is the most commercially useful thing that can be built from it? The honest starting position is weaker than it was for the credit engine. The bottom-up engine is a designed framework, not a validated one: the base-rate test (tab 13, item 1) has not been run, there are no backtested moat scores, no live picks, and no track record of any kind. Its core production step — partner judgment applied one company at a time through the Firm Lens — does not scale the way the quant engine's SQL does; every deep-dive consumes hours from the scarcest input the shop has. What is on hand and genuinely rare: a survivorship-bias-free fundamentals panel (17,759 firms including delisted names back to 1990 via Sharadar), which happens to be exactly the substrate the matched-loser discipline (Guard A) requires and which most retail-facing incumbents do not have; a working backtest engine and regime machinery to quantify the framework's filterable half; and an existing publishing pipeline. The competitive landscape is crowded and famous — Morningstar's moat ratings feed an $11B ETF, Motley Fool has run the multibagger-subscription slot for 30 years, and the 2024–26 AI-research wave (AlphaSense, Fiscal.ai, Fintool — now Microsoft's) is compressing the price of generic company analysis toward zero. Each option below is scored against that reality, not against what a funded startup could do. The one asset nobody in the landscape sells is the matched-loser base-rate library — "here are the companies that looked identical to the winner and died" — and most of the appendix's value concentrates around building exactly that.

How the raw material becomes products

Three core assets, none of which exists today, sit between the framework and every sellable product. The first two (base-rate library, moat-score panel) are one research program viewed from two angles; the third (a live sleeve with a track record) is the only thing that converts qualitative claims into credibility. As with the credit engine, the marginal cost of a second product once the assets exist is mostly distribution — the actual scarce resource.

The ten options

1 · Internal First: the BU Engine as Shelf-4 Sleeve of the Meta-Engine Internal use · hybrid quant-screen → moat deep-dive (meta-engine Shelf-4 Avenues 4/5) · zero new data Run the framework on the shop's own capital: a quantified Firm-Lens pre-screen feeding 15–25 manual deep-dives, held as a concentrated sleeve inside the blend, sized by the regime meta-engine.
LOW–MED⌄
What it is

The tab-10/11 hand-off made real. Layer 1: a quant pre-screen over the Sharadar universe implementing the computable half of the Firm Lens — ROIC level and direction, reinvestment rate, revenue growth vs. dilution, gross-margin trajectory, insider ownership, share-count discipline — the filters, cheaply. Layer 2: manual Scorecard + Guard-B deep-dives on the ~50 survivors, converging to a 15–25 name concentrated sleeve. Layer 3: position sizing wired to the existing DRN/HML regime machinery and fractional-Kelly framework (exponentials capped in frenzies, compounders leaned into in golden ages — tab 11 as a formula). This is precisely the "hybrid quant-screen → discretionary deep-dive" avenue sketched for Shelf 4 of the meta-engine.

Work involved

Weeks, not months, for layer 1: every input is already in master_prices_computed / Sharadar fundamentals, and the backtest engine runs the screen historically out of the box. The honest gaps: runway/TAM has no clean Sharadar field (proxy via revenue vs. sector aggregates, market-share-of-industry-revenue — imperfect, disclose it); NRR/CAC (the M2/M4 signatures) are not in structured data at all and stay manual. Layer 2 is the permanent cost: 15–25 deep-dives plus quarterly maintenance is real recurring partner time — budget 1–2 days per new name, half a day per quarterly review. Layer 3 reuses the blend/Kelly code as-is.

Difficulty — Low–Medium

All infrastructure exists; the difficulty is discipline (the deep-dive cadence) and the ordinary research risk that the quantified filters add nothing over the existing LV/quality tilts.

Form of end product

A live concentrated sleeve in the blended portfolio + the backtestable "Firm-Lens screen" as a new strategy in the library. No external surface.

Addressable market

None by design — the customer is the shop's own book. The strategic value: this is the only option that manufactures a track record, and a track record is the gating asset for options 4, 7, 8, and 10. It is also the only honest way to discover whether the framework's discriminators (moat direction, runway, Builder, survival) actually discriminate when applied prospectively rather than to a hand-picked pantheon.

Commercial potential

No direct revenue; value arrives as return on live capital plus validation. Time to first dollar: immediate in expectation once live. Key risks: concentrated books can underperform for years even when right (the framework itself demands holding through 2–3 gut-wrenching drawdowns); a bad first two years poisons every downstream option that leans on the record. Mitigation: run the quant-screen layer as a paper/backtest strategy first and publish the methodology before the results.

Competitive positioning

Internally this competes only with the shop's own quant sleeves for capital. Externally it is the two-person version of what Akre (three-legged stool: compounding businesses, skilled managers as partners, reinvestment at above-average returns), Giverny, Woodlock House (Chris Mayer, ~10 names) and Fundsmith (~£20B-scale, "buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing") run at scale — all of which prove the strategy class works and none of which is a competitor to an internal book. The differentiator vs. all of them is the regime-conditioned sizing layer: none of the quality-compounder shops sizes by macro regime; the framework's tab-11 hand-off is genuinely unusual.

v0 spec — what ships first

(a) SQL screen: domestic/no-ADR/no-SPAC prefilter → ROIC > 15% and rising 3y, reinvestment rate > 30%, revenue CAGR > 10%, net dilution < 2%/yr, FCF-positive or funded-for-3y; (b) backtest 2001–2021 IS with the standard IS/OOS discipline, benchmark vs. RSP/VW per the EW-inflation rule; (c) score the top 20 current survivors on the eight Scorecard columns; (d) paper sleeve, monthly marks, DRN/HML-conditioned weights. Ships in 2–4 weeks of part-time work.

Go-to-market

Not applicable — but everything produced here (screen methodology, scored names, sleeve performance) is reusable marketing raw material for options 2 and 4. Write it up as if it will be published, from day one.

2 · Annual "Compounders & Impostors" Study — the Published Base-Rate Test Brand-builder · runs the roadmap's Guard-A base-rate test on Sharadar and publishes it free, annually The Alta Fox / Mauboussin / Verdad play: a rigorous, free, annually-updated study of multibagger winners vs. matched losers with survivorship-corrected base rates — the marketing engine for everything else.
LOW–MED⌄
What it is

Tab 13's first roadmap item, packaged for the world: take every US-listed company since 1990, define multibaggers (e.g. ≥10× total return over rolling 10-year windows), and — the differentiator — for each winner construct the matched cohort: every company that shared the winner's visible profile at the decision date (size, growth, margins, sector, era) and measure how many won, how many died, and which features had lift. Published free as an annual HTML/PDF study: base-rate tables ("of the 214 companies that looked like NVDA-2013, 9 became 10-baggers and 61 delisted"), the Type-I gallery updated with fresh corpses, and a scored forward watchlist. The Sharadar delisted coverage makes this honest in a way most retail studies are not.

Work involved

A real research program but entirely inside existing competence: define multibagger events, build the matching engine (nearest-neighbor on point-in-time fundamentals — the same discipline as the strategy backtests), compute feature lift per Scorecard column analog, stress the matching choices. First edition: 1–3 months of part-time work. Annual updates: days. Writing and figures ride the existing Railway publishing stack. No licensing issue for published aggregate statistics and analytics derived from Sharadar (unlike raw-data redistribution — same check as the credit appendix's Option 1, get it in writing).

Difficulty — Low–Medium

Methodologically it is a matched-cohort study, which the shop already knows how to do; the risk is a null or embarrassing result — e.g. the framework's favorite features show no lift. That risk is also the point.

Form of end product

Free annual study (HTML on the existing stack + PDF), heavy on figures and tables; a condensed thread/summary for distribution; the underlying pair database retained as Option 3's asset.

Addressable market

Not sold — attention is the product. The precedents are strong: Alta Fox's "Makings of a Multibagger" (2020, 645 slides, built largely by interns: 104 stocks >350% TSR 2015–20, avg +922% vs. SPX +55%) became one of the most-shared buyside documents of its year and put a small Fort Worth fund on the map. Mauboussin's Base Rate Book (Credit Suisse, 2016 — base rates for sales-growth persistence, the direct intellectual comp for this study's denominator discipline) is still cited a decade later. Verdad built ~$1B AUM substantially on a free weekly research letter. Bessembinder (4% of stocks account for all net wealth creation since 1926, across 26,168 firms) proves the topic has academic legs.

Commercial potential

$0 direct, permanently — and that is fine. It is the credibility engine: the subscription (4), the database (3), the course/book (9), and the advisory (10) all convert its audience. Time to indirect dollars: 6–12 months via option 4. Key risks: it simply doesn't get read (distribution is earned, not automatic); or the base rates contradict the framework — in which case the framework gets revised, which is Guard A doing its job in public and, handled honestly, builds more trust than a confirming result.

3 · The Matched-Loser / Base-Rate Database as a Product Data product · Option 2's pair engine, productized · the survivorship-bias-corrected pattern library "Show me every company that looked like this one at its stage — and what happened to all of them." A queryable point-in-time cohort database nobody currently sells.
MEDIUM⌄
What it is

Option 2's internal engine exposed as a product: give it a ticker (or a profile — size, growth, margins, sector, stage), and it returns the historical cohort of companies that matched that profile at their own decision dates, with full outcome distributions: % 10-baggers, % delisted/acquired/zombie, median forward return, and which subsequent features separated the tails. It is the "outside view" (Kahneman via Mauboussin) as an API call — the honest denominator for any growth story. Nobody sells this: Morningstar sells present-tense moat opinions, screeners sell present-tense fundamentals, and academics publish unconditional aggregates.

Work involved

Beyond Option 2: harden the matching methodology (choice of features, calipers, era-conditioning — the intellectual content); build a thin query API + web front end; write validation documentation showing cohort outcomes are stable out-of-sample and matching isn't overfit; a Sharadar derived-data licensing check (outcome statistics and cohort lists are analytics, not raw redistribution — but confirm in writing). Incremental months over Option 2, most of it methodology-hardening rather than plumbing.

Difficulty — Medium

The build is modest once Option 2 exists; the difficulty is that this is a genuinely new product category, so every sale requires educating the buyer on why denominators matter — a slow, missionary sell.

Form of end product

Two skins: (a) a professional flat-file/API for funds ($5–25K/yr); (b) a retail web lookup ("base-rate check this ticker") at $10–30/mo, plausibly embedded inside Option 4's subscription rather than sold alone.

Addressable market

Fundamental long-only funds and family offices that already consume Mauboussin-style work; equity-research teams wanting an outside-view exhibit in every initiation memo; business-school cases; sophisticated retail. No direct incumbent. Adjacent players who could clone it fast if it works: Morningstar (data + analysts), Verdad (loves base rates, publishes free), academic-data shops. Honest sizing: the professional buyer pool that pays for a novel single-purpose dataset from an unknown shop is small — tens of firms at best in the first years.

Commercial potential

Rough estimate: $10–75K/yr by year 2–3 (2–5 professional clients plus a thin retail tail), with real odds of near-zero standalone and healthier odds as Option 4's differentiating feature. Time to first dollar: 12–18 months (after Option 2 v1 ships and travels). Key risks: category-creation burden; free-rider problem (the annual study gives away the headline findings — deliberate tension: the study sells the depth); clone risk once proven.

Competitive positioning

Closest analogues, none of which is the product: Morningstar moat ratings (871 US names, present-tense, analyst opinion, no outcome denominators); Verdad's research (base-rate-flavored, free, not queryable); Mauboussin/Counterpoint Global publications (free PDFs, unconditional); backtesting platforms (Portfolio123 etc. — user must build the study themselves); AlphaSense/Fiscal.ai (retrieval and summarization, not survivorship-corrected cohorts — their LLMs are trained on a survivor-heavy corpus, which is exactly the bias this product corrects). Positioning line: "Every research tool tells you about the company. This one tells you about the reference class."

v0 spec — what ships first

A single-page web tool on the existing stack: ticker in → cohort card out (n matched at analogous stage, outcome histogram, top-lift features, 5 named winners / 5 named losers from the cohort). Powered by a nightly-refreshed static table, no realtime compute. Free for 25 famous tickers as the demo; everything else behind Option 4's paywall or a $15/mo standalone gate.

Go-to-market

Launch inside Option 2's study ("the database behind this study is queryable"). Professional tier sold by direct outreach to the funds that cite Mauboussin/Alta Fox publicly — a literal list of a few hundred names — with the annual study as the credential. No paid acquisition; this either travels on novelty or it isn't good enough.

What it is

The established multibagger-newsletter category, entered with a structurally different pitch. Every incumbent sells conviction; this sells conviction with denominators: each idea arrives as a full Scorecard (moats + direction + runway + Builder + signatures + survival), its Guard-A cohort ("companies that looked like this: X% 10-bagged, Y% died"), a named matched loser and the specific variable separating them, and pre-committed decay/sell triggers (tab 9D). Monthly cadence: screen refresh + 2 deep-dives + quarterly scorecard-change reviews. The regime meta-engine adds a sizing note no retail letter has ("the macro engine currently reads frenzy: halve position sizes, raise the Guard-A bar").

Work involved

Modeling: options 1–2's outputs reused wholesale. Product: Stripe + gated pages on the Railway stack (commodity); templated deep-dive format (LLM-assisted drafting from screen output and filings, human-scored and human-signed — Guard B applies to ourselves). The dominant cost is the same one the credit appendix flagged for its research option: a permanent editorial cadence tax on two people, ~2–4 days/month forever, and a mandatory 6–12 month free period publishing tracked picks before anyone should or will pay.

Difficulty — Medium

Zero technical risk; the entire difficulty is audience-building in a brutally crowded category, plus the reputational exposure of public picks in a strategy class where being right can take five years.

Form of end product

Substack-style subscription: free tier (screen summaries, study excerpts) + paid tier $200–400/yr (full Scorecards, cohort cards, watchlist, archive). Possibly a later "pro" tier with the Option 3 database bundled at $1–2K/yr.

Compounding Quality (one Belgian ex-asset-manager on Substack): 567K subscribers, #6 in Substack finance — built in ~3 years. Substack overall: 8.4M paid subscriptions platform-wide, 50+ authors above $1M ARR. The market exists at every scale; the question is only what slice an unknown shop earns.

Commercial potential

Rough estimate: $20–120K/yr by year 2–3 (100–400 paid at ~$250 avg), with the top decile of outcomes far higher if Option 2's study travels (the Compounding Quality path exists but is lottery-adjacent). Time to first dollar: 3–6 months, meaningful revenue after the credibility year. Key risks: churn if early picks stumble (concentrated multibagger picks will stumble — the base-rate framing is partly a churn defense, set expectations with denominators); cadence tax; publisher's-exemption hygiene (impersonal research, no personalized advice — same check as the credit appendix).

5 · Systematic Moat-Score Dataset / API (the "Morningstar Moat Ratings at Scale" Play) Data product · LLM-assisted scoring of filings across the full Sharadar universe, historical and point-in-time · validation-gated Machine-scored M1–M8 moat presence, stack count, and direction for ~5,000 active + thousands of delisted US firms, with history — licensable the way Morningstar licenses moat ratings, if and only if the scores prove predictive.
HIGH⌄
What it is

Morningstar's moat rating is ~871 US stocks, analyst-produced, present-tense, and famously licensable (it powers an $11B ETF). This option builds the systematic version: an LLM pipeline reads 10-K/10-Q text (business description, competition, risk factors, MD&A) plus structured fundamentals, and emits the framework's schema — which of M1–M8 are present, stack count, and the decisive direction field — for the whole Sharadar universe, including delisted names, reconstructed historically (score 2005 filings with 2005-visible information). The historical, survivorship-free panel is the differentiator: it enables the one thing Morningstar's product can't do — backtesting moat claims — and doubles as Options 2/3's feature set.

Work involved

The heaviest build in this appendix. EDGAR full-text ingestion (free, real engineering); prompt/rubric design per moat type with the framework's "trap" columns as negative criteria; calibration against known cases; LLM cost for ~25 years × thousands of firms × multiple filings (manageable with batching/caching but nontrivial); then the gating item — a validation study proving the scores predict forward ROIC persistence and excess returns out-of-sample. One deep methodological trap deserves its own sentence: hindsight contamination — the LLM knows Nvidia won and Blockbuster died, so naive historical scoring is leakage by construction. Mitigations (mask identifiers, restrict inputs to filing text, test on post-training-cutoff filings, compare against name-visible runs to measure the contamination) are real work and must be documented like a backtest-leakage audit, because buyers will probe exactly this.

Difficulty — High

Not the plumbing — the proof. An unvalidated moat score is an opinion at scale; a validated one is an alpha claim, and alpha claims from unknown shops face maximal skepticism. The hindsight-contamination problem is unsolved in public literature.

Form of end product

Flat-file/API subscription: current scores + the historical point-in-time panel (the lead asset for quant buyers, exactly as the credit appendix concluded for the PD panel), plus per-score evidence excerpts (the filing passages that triggered each moat call) for auditability.

Addressable market

Quant equity funds shopping for differentiated quality/alt-data factors (alt-data budgets are real; single-dataset price points typically $10–100K/yr); fundamental funds wanting screening leverage; index/ETF sponsors (feeds Option 8); retail platforms as licensees. Incumbents on the "moat as data" shelf: Morningstar (analyst moat ratings — narrow coverage, no history-as-data, licensed at institutional prices and to its own index arm: Morningstar Indexes runs ~$90M/yr revenue); MSCI/S&P quality indices (accounting-ratio quality, no moat semantics; QUAL $44.6B AUM at 0.15%, SPHQ 0.15% — proof the adjacent factor is a commodity); hobbyist-scale web scorers (Find My Moat) — no institutional product between "four analysts' opinions" and "ROE ratios," which is the wedge.

Commercial potential

Rough estimate: $0 until validated; $50–250K/yr by year 3 if validation is strong (a handful of quant buyers at $15–50K plus retail-platform licensing); tail outcome much larger via Option 8. Time to first dollar: 12–24 months. Key risks: validation fails or is ambiguous (scores describe rather than predict — the framework itself warns "has a moat" had near-zero lift; the product must score direction, the hard part); hindsight contamination undermines the historical panel's credibility; LLM-vendor cost/behavior drift; incumbents (Morningstar, MSCI, AlphaSense) can build the same pipeline with more data and brand the moment it looks valuable.

6 · AI Moat-Analyst Copilot ("Ticker in → Firm Lens out") SaaS · LLM + the framework as a rubric + Options 3/5 as grounding · crowded category, wedge is the opinionated output Upload a ticker; get the full Firm-Lens analysis — Scorecard, Guard A/B checks, base-rate cohort, matched losers, sell triggers — in minutes, grounded in filings and the shop's databases.
MED–HIGH⌄
What it is

A structured-output copilot, not a chat box: the user names a company; the system runs the framework end-to-end — filters (runway proxy, industry, price gate), the eight Scorecard columns with filing citations, Guard B's promoter-vs-allocator facts (insider ownership, allocation record), the Option 3 cohort card, and a named matched loser with the separating variable. Work involved

EDGAR retrieval + prompt/template engineering (shared with Option 5's pipeline — build once); hard hallucination control: every number injected from the database by construction, never generated (the credit-copilot lesson verbatim); the Guard-B fact sheet requires structured insider-ownership and capital-allocation data (Sharadar covers some; SEC Form 4 ingestion is the gap); front end + billing. Months of part-time work if Option 5's pipeline exists; not worth starting before it.

Difficulty — Medium–High

Known engineering; the problem is the category. 2024–26 produced a wave of AI equity-research products with more funding and distribution, and the incumbents are absorbing the feature set (Fintool acquired by Microsoft in 2026; Fiscal.ai shipping inside ChatGPT as an official app).

Form of end product

Web app: ticker → rendered Firm-Lens report (HTML/PDF), $20–50/mo prosumer; or an API returning the structured Scorecard JSON. Most realistically: the interactive skin of Option 4's subscription rather than a standalone company.

Addressable market

Prosumer/retail investors (the Fiscal.ai / TIKR audience: $130–600/yr price tolerance) and small fundamental funds priced out of AlphaSense ($10–20K/seat, enterprise deals $50–100K+). Incumbents and their pricing: AlphaSense ($10–20K/seat), Fiscal.ai (ex-FinChat; Pro $39/mo, $10M Series A, now an official ChatGPT app), Fintool (institutional AI analyst — acquired by Microsoft, v5 builds DCFs and memos autonomously), Daloopa (enterprise data extraction, custom pricing), Quartr (IR/transcript AI, Pro via sales). All of them summarize; none scores against an investment framework with base rates — that is the whole wedge, and it is a feature-sized wedge, not a company-sized one.

Commercial potential

Standalone: $0–80K/yr, 12+ months to first dollar, high probability the generic layer is commoditized to zero by incumbents' bundles. Key risks: LLM output quality on the judgment-heavy columns (Builder, counter-positioning) embarrasses the brand — mitigate by labeling machine sections vs. partner-reviewed sections; per-query cost vs. $30/mo pricing; incumbent bundling speed.

The honest corollary from the credit appendix's identical analysis (its Option 8) applies unchanged: a two-person copilot survives only where it is grounded in engines the incumbents don't have — here, the Guard-A cohort database and the validated moat-direction scores. Without Options 3/5 underneath it, this is a prompt wrapper and should not be built.

v0 spec — what ships first

Not a product — a demo: 10 pre-rendered Firm-Lens reports (5 pantheon names, 5 current watchlist names) published as HTML, machine-generated and partner-annotated, with a "request a ticker" email form to measure demand before writing a line of app code.

Go-to-market

Inside Option 4's funnel exclusively. If standalone demand shows up in the request logs, revisit; do not market it independently against nine-figure-funded incumbents.

7 · Concentrated Compounder Model Portfolio / SMA for RIAs Asset management · Option 1's sleeve, externalized · gated on registration, track record, and platform gatekeepers The 15–25 name compounder strategy distributed as a strategist model on RIA platforms (Envestnet, Schwab Model Market Center, Vestmark) or as an SMA — the largest honest market in this appendix, and the slowest to enter.
HIGH⌄
What it is

Positioning: difficulty vs. commercial potential (bubble = addressable market)

Positions are judgment calls, not measurements. Recommended build sequence

Build Options 1 and 2 together, starting now; they are one research program with two outputs. The quantified Firm-Lens screen, the paper/live sleeve, and the Guard-A matched-loser study share the same code, the same Sharadar substrate, and the same point-in-time discipline the shop already practices daily. Option 1 manufactures the track record; Option 2 manufactures the audience — and Option 2 is also the framework's own scientific due diligence: if moat-direction, runway, survival, and Builder proxies show no lift over matched losers, better to learn that in month three than after a subscription launches. Everything else in this appendix is gated on one or both of those assets.

Then let the options compound instead of compete. Option 4 (the subscription) launches on the study's audience with a free-first year and the matched-loser exhibit as its differentiator; Option 3 (the database) ships as its premium feature before it ships as a standalone API; Option 9 (course/book) harvests the same audience with cadence-free products. Option 5 (the moat-score panel) is the one genuinely new bet worth real hours — run its v0 validation as an appendix to the second annual study, and let the result decide whether the dataset, the copilot (6), and the index play (8) exist at all. Options 7 and 10 are standing options: keep the sleeve's marks clean and the process document current (7), take pulled advisory work at good rates (10), build neither on spec. The through-line is the same one the credit appendix landed on: use the framework on your own money first; sell the exhaust.

Cross-references to the top-down engines' commercial appendix. The BU engine's options are worth more jointly than alone: (a) validated moat/quality scores (Option 5) drop into the existing quant screens as a universe filter — the natural first test is whether a moat-direction tilt improves LV (whose edge concentrates in low-beta Q1 names) or protects MOM's tail the way the credit PD screen was slated to; (b) the compounder sleeve (Option 1) is the designed occupant of Shelf 4's hybrid avenue in the meta-engine, sized by the same DRN/HML/Kelly machinery as the blend — one more sleeve in an architecture built for exactly this; (c) the publishing/subscription infrastructure, Sharadar-derived-data licensing check, publisher's-exemption hygiene, and "open validation as credibility" playbook are shared line items — whichever engine's research product launches first amortizes them for the other; and (d) the two annual studies (credit watchlists, compounder base rates) are one editorial franchise to a largely overlapping audience. A two-person shop cannot run ten products; it can run one research program whose exhaust feeds four or five surfaces.
