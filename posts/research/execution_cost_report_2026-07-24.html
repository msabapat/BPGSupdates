What this measures

Every market centre must publish, monthly, the execution quality it delivered — broken out per ticker, per order type, and per order size bucket. This report uses the two venues that together handle the largest share of E*TRADE order flow (Citadel ~25–29%, HRT ~16–20%).

Effective spread — the cost actually paid, not the posted quote.
S = 2 × |P − M| / M    where P = trade price, M = quote midpoint
Worked example: stock quoted 49.98 bid / 50.02 ask, so M = 50.00. You buy at P = 50.01 (inside the quote — price improvement). Then S = 2 × |50.01 − 50.00| / 50.00 = 0.0004 = 4 bps. Had you paid the full ask of 50.02, S = 2 × 0.02/50 = 8 bps — the full quoted width.
Why effective spread ≈ round-trip cost. One-way cost is |P−M|/M, i.e. half the effective spread. A round trip (buy then sell) pays that twice — so it equals S itself. That makes these numbers directly comparable to our TC_RATE = 0.0005 = 5 bps per side = 10 bps round-trip.
bps (basis points) — 1 bp = 0.01%. So 10 bps = 0.10% = $1,000 on a $1,000,000 trade.
Order size buckets — Rule 605's four share-count categories: 21 = 100–499 sh 22 = 500–1,999 23 = 2,000–4,999 24 = 5,000–9,999
Bucket 24 is the largest that exists: the rule defines "covered orders" as those under 10,000 shares, so nothing bigger is ever reported.
Slope (our shorthand in this report) — the ratio bucket 24 cost ÷ bucket 21 cost, i.e. how many times more expensive the largest observable order is versus the smallest.
Worked example: STRL costs 6.75 bps at 100–499 sh and 47.74 bps at 5,000–9,999 sh on Citadel → slope = 47.74 / 6.75 = 7.1×.

Method

  • Order types: market (code 11) and marketable-limit (12) only — the liquidity-demanding orders. Non-marketable limit orders (13/14/15) provide liquidity and their spread means something different.
  • Share-weighted: each monthly cell weighted by shares executed, so a month with heavy volume counts proportionally more than a thin one.
  • Prices: dollar spreads converted to bps using the historical monthly average close for each ticker over the matching month (from daily_equity_prices), not today's price — so a name that moved 40% over the year is converted correctly month by month.
  • Sample filter: a (ticker, bucket) cell needs ≥20,000 shares over the 12 months to be included, dropping thin-sample noise. 404 of 444 names survive with data at both the smallest and largest bucket on both venues.

Headline finding

Cost rises steeply and monotonically with order size — roughly 4.1× from the smallest to the largest observable bucket. At the largest bucket the median name costs 8.88 bps, essentially at our 10 bps assumption, and 181 of 404 names (45%) exceed it.

This refines the earlier conclusion, it doesn't contradict it. The prior figure (~1.7 bps median, "TC_RATE is conservative") was share-weighted across all order sizes and was therefore dominated by small retail orders, which genuinely are that cheap. Once you condition on size, the gradient appears. Both numbers are correct — they answer different questions.

Slope vs. absolute level — which names actually matter

A steep slope alone is not a danger signal. The steepest slopes in the whole sample belong to mega-caps that start almost free: NVDA runs 0.14 → 0.93 bps (6.6×), VRT 0.48 → 11.9 bps (24.8×). Multiplying a near-zero base by 20 still leaves a small number.

The ceiling — why every number here is a floor

Rule 605 "covered orders" exclude anything at or above 10,000 shares. Bucket 24 (5,000–9,999 sh) is therefore the largest order size that exists anywhere in this dataset.

In dollar terms that ceiling is small — median ~$895,000 of notional (P25 $449K, P75 $1.78M) across these names. Against our own position sizes at a 10% weight:

Critically, the cost curve is still rising at the ceiling — median 4.1× with no sign of flattening between buckets 23 and 24. So these figures are lower bounds, not estimates of what we would pay at our sizes.

Other caveats

  • Two venues, not all seven. Citadel and HRT are the two largest destinations for E*TRADE flow but together are roughly 45–50% of it. G1, Virtu, Jane Street, Two Sigma and GTS are not included here.
  • Retail flow, not ours. These are the executions those venues gave all their order flow, predominantly retail. Our order profile differs.
  • Not depth. This is realised cost by size, not order-book depth. The only depth data we hold is a single ~5-minute top-of-book snapshot from 23 Jul 2026, which is too thin to characterise typical depth.
  • 12 months, not 3 years. The 36-month reach comes from the ticker universe (every name held over 3 years); the execution data itself is 12 months.
